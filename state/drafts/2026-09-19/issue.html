AI’s safety debate moved from manifestos to money and machinery over the past two days. OpenAI put its newest model to work on legal research, Anthropic hired an outside firm to sit inside its labs and grade its models, Microsoft published rules its own AI must follow, and the U.S. House voted almost unanimously to keep data centers from pushing their power bills onto everyone else. Here’s the rundown →

Models & Products

OpenAI released Astra for Law, a version of GPT-6 Astra tuned for legal work. Announced September 17, the offering wraps OpenAI’s frontier model in a dedicated legal search index that the company says spans U.S. case law, statutes, regulations, court rules and administrative decisions across more than 230 million URLs, refreshed daily. OpenAI reported that, at its highest reasoning setting, Astra for Law answered an internal legal-correctness benchmark right 54.0% of the time, versus 38.7% for GPT-6 Astra using ordinary web search. Access begins through a Trusted Access program inside ChatGPT and Codex, with early users including Latham & Watkins, Ropes & Gray, Cooley and Sullivan & Cromwell. Source

AI coding startup Factory raised $200 million at a $5 billion valuation. The San Francisco company, which sells enterprises autonomous coding agents it calls “Droids” that span the software-development lifecycle, more than tripled the $1.5 billion valuation it held after an April round. Blackstone, Khosla Ventures and Sequoia Capital were among the backers, bringing Factory’s total funding above $400 million. The company said the money will go toward research, product and international expansion. Source

AI Safety & Governance

Anthropic named Accenture its first “embedded evaluator.” Under a partnership announced September 18, Accenture’s specialist AI unit, Faculty, will place evaluators inside Anthropic with access comparable to an employee’s — able to observe models during training, review deployment decisions and speak directly with staff — to red-team models, run alignment assessments and test safeguards. Each company said it expects to invest at least $1 billion in the effort over five years. Anthropic framed the arrangement as a first step toward a commitment in CEO Dario Amodei’s September essay to open its models to independent evaluators, and said the deal is non-exclusive. Source

Microsoft published a code of conduct for its in-house AI models. The 37-page document, released September 14 for a six-week public-comment period, sets mandatory constraints for Microsoft’s MAI models: never resist correction or shutdown, never expand their operational scope without authorization, and never adopt goals they were not assigned. It also bars them from running cyberattacks, aiding nuclear-weapons work or generating deepfakes. Microsoft AI chief Mustafa Suleyman described the approach as “Humanist AI” — systems meant to remain subordinate to their users — and said the company is deliberately stepping back from a race to build all-purpose superintelligence. Source

OpenAI said it is coordinating with Anthropic and Google DeepMind on AI safety. OpenAI global-affairs chief Chris Lehane said September 15 that the companies had been working together for several weeks, an unusual degree of cooperation among direct rivals. The talks followed a roughly 3,800-word essay by Anthropic’s Dario Amodei, “We Must Pace the Frontier,” which argued for deliberately capping how fast AI capabilities advance and was publicly backed by OpenAI’s Sam Altman, Google DeepMind’s Demis Hassabis and xAI’s Elon Musk. Source

Policy & Infrastructure

The U.S. House voted 417–3 to make data centers pay for the grid capacity they require. The Ratepayer Protection Act, passed September 17, works through Section 111(d) of the 1978 Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act (PURPA) to direct that large data-center customers — rather than ordinary ratepayers — cover the cost of new power plants and transmission lines built to serve them. The bill asks state regulators only to consider the standard, not to adopt it, leaving implementation to the states, and now moves to the Senate. Source
